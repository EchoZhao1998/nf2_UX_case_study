*EIHUB Challenge 2026 · Vision to Venture · Wanting (Echo) Zhao*

---

## How this is built

**Timing.** 640 words. At a deliberate 135 words/minute that lands at **~4:45**, leaving 15 seconds of margin. Two lines are marked ✂ — cut them if you are running long; nothing downstream depends on them.

**Structure.** Follows the organisers' recommended flow (empathy → depth of problem → the idea → invitation), while covering all five Idea Proposal areas. Personal introduction is *woven into the opening* rather than given its own block — for this idea, your lived experience IS the empathy hook, and spending a separate 30 seconds on credentials would waste your strongest 15 seconds.

**On-screen text.** Each slide carries one short line — the sentence you are saying at that moment. It anchors you if you lose your place, and it means a judge who mishears still gets the point.

## Pitch Strategy

**Target delivery:** ~4:15–4:30, leaving room for pauses and Q&A.

**Structure:**

**Core message:**

> **MediPort helps patients turn scattered medical records into something they can actually use in a consultation.**

# The Script

## ⏱ 0:00 — Slide 1 · The Problem

Hi, I'm Echo. I have NF2, a rare disease, and I lost my hearing because of it.

Let me show you what happens in a typical five-minute hospital consultation.

I have to explain years of medical history, across different hospitals, from a bag of paper reports and MRI films.

And I may be trying to lip-read a doctor at the same time.

The problem is simple:

**My medical information is scattered, but I'm expected to connect it.**

---

## ⏱ 0:40 — Slide 2 · Why This Matters

This isn't just my problem.

I surveyed 38 NF2 patients and caregivers.

**Zero had a tool designed to bring their records together.**

66% still rely on paper or films.  
71% reported communication problems with a new doctor.  
And 76% reported hearing loss.

So the gap isn't storage. We already have cloud drives and hospital apps.

The gap is what happens **between hospitals, during the consultation.**

---

## ⏱ 1:25 — Slide 3 · The Solution

That's why I'm building **MediPort**.

The idea is simple:

**Collect first. Organise later.**

After a hospital visit, you photograph the records you receive.

MediPort extracts the key information and puts it into one timeline — hospital, date, diagnosis, results.

Before the next consultation, one tap creates a doctor-ready summary: key history, latest results, and the patient's questions.

And every important number links back to the original record, so the doctor can check it.

---

## ⏱ 2:15 — Slide 4 · The Payoff

The important change is this:

**I don't have to narrate my medical history. I can show it.**

A doctor can see the important information quickly, instead of trying to reconstruct it from five minutes of conversation.

For someone with hearing loss, this also changes the interaction.

I can use the timeline and written information instead of relying only on spoken communication.

**The record becomes part of the conversation.**

---

## ⏱ 2:55 — Slide 5 · Why MediPort?

So why not just use Google Drive, a hospital app, or an existing health-record app?

Because they solve different problems.

Hospital apps keep information inside one hospital.

Cloud drives store files, but don't turn them into a usable medical timeline.

Manual record apps depend on patients entering everything themselves.

MediPort is designed around the gap between hospitals:

**Patient-owned. Longitudinal. Accessibility-first. Consultation-ready.**

---

## ⏱ 3:30 — Slide 6 · Feasibility

This doesn't require new science.

The core technology already exists:

**Camera capture → OCR → AI extraction → Timeline → Summary**

My first MVP would focus on one thing:

Turning messy records into a reliable, traceable timeline and summary.

And there is an important boundary:

**MediPort organises information. It does not diagnose or give medical advice.**

---

## ⏱ 3:55 — Slide 7 · Business & Scale

The first users are NF2 patients and caregivers, but the problem is much bigger.

The business model is simple:

Free basic capture, then a low-cost subscription for AI organisation and family sharing.

There is also potential for sponsorship through patient organisations and insurers, because better record coordination can reduce unnecessary duplication and wasted effort.

**NF2 is the starting point.**

The same problem exists for anyone receiving care across multiple hospitals.

---

## ⏱ 4:20 — Slide 8 · The Ask

Next, I want to test one critical question:

**Can a clinician actually use the summary in a real five-minute consultation?**

I plan to test this with patients, clinicians, and a rare-disease organisation.

What I need from Monash is help connecting with clinical and patient partners, and support to take the validated prototype into a pilot.

My goal is to turn a workaround I built for myself into a tool that can help many people.

Thank you.

## The script

### ⏱ 0:00 — Slide 1

My name is Echo. I have a rare disease called NF2, and I lost my hearing to it as an adult.

I want to tell you about five minutes.

### ⏱ 0:15 — Slide 2

So what actually breaks here?

Not storage. Cloud drives already exist. What breaks is *transfer*. The information a doctor needs in the first minute is locked in formats that cannot survive five minutes: paper, film, and memory.

And the cost is not abstract. Scans get repeated because the last one can't be found. Families travel a thousand kilometres for a consult that fails on communication. ✂ *Decisions — operate, or wait — get made on partial history.*

Institutions own the records. Patients own the consequences.

### ⏱ 1:45 — Slide 4

For years I thought this was my personal failure. So I surveyed my own community — 38 patients and caregivers.

Not one of them had a tool for this. Zero.

Two-thirds keep paper and film. The rest keep nothing at all. Seventy-one percent break down communicating with a new doctor. Seventy-six percent are losing their hearing, like me.

This isn't my problem. It's a designed absence.

### ⏱ 2:15 — Slide 5

MediPort is built on one principle: **collect first, organise later.**

Step one — after any hospital visit, you photograph whatever you were handed. No forms. No typing.

Step two — the system extracts it and places it on one timeline. Which hospital, which date, which numbers.

Step three — before your next consult, one tap generates a doctor-ready summary. Diagnosis, key events, latest results, your questions. Bilingual. And every number on it taps back to the original film — so the doctor can trust it.

### ⏱ 3:00 — Slide 6

Here is what that changes.

I no longer narrate. I show. The doctor reads in fifteen seconds what used to take me three minutes to say badly.

Hospital apps stop at the hospital. Cloud drives store files but give you no timeline and no summary. Existing record apps demand manual entry — which is exactly why my 38 respondents use none of them.

Nobody has designed for the consultation itself. And nobody has designed it for people who can't hear.

That combination is the gap: patient-owned, longitudinal, accessibility-first, consult-ready.

## The deck — 10 slides

**Rule of the deck:** one idea per slide, one line of text, one image. The judges should be *listening* to you, not reading. Every slide below fits on a single line of on-screen text — if you find yourself adding a second bullet, that's a new slide or a cut.

### Slide 1 · Title — the hook
- **On screen:** `MediPort` / subtitle: *Five minutes to explain fifteen years.*
- **Visual:** Full-bleed dark background. Nothing else. No logo grid, no name card clutter — your name is spoken.
- **Why:** The subtitle plants the "five minutes" frame that the whole pitch pays off. It also quietly mirrors the pitch length — judges will notice.

### Slide 2 · The five minutes — empathy
- **On screen:** *A five-minute consult. A bag of films. A masked doctor I can't hear.*
- **Visual:** A photograph — the plastic bag of films and paper reports. If you have a real one from your own care, **use it**; an authentic photo of your own records beats any stock image or icon in this deck. Shoot it on a plain surface, overhead, natural light.
- **Why:** This is your single strongest emotional asset. Give it a whole slide and hold the silence for a beat after "wearing a mask."

### Slide 3 · Root cause + hidden cost
- **On screen:** *Institutions own the records. Patients own the consequences.*
- **Visual:** A simple diagram — three hospital boxes, each with a closed boundary, and one small patient figure walking between them carrying everything. Hand-drawn feel is fine and reads as honest.
- **Why:** This slide is where judges decide whether you *understand* the problem or just *have* it. The diagram proves systemic thinking; the closing line is quotable.

### Slide 4 · The evidence
- **On screen:** *0 of 38.*
- **Visual:** Enormous "0 / 38" as the hero number. Beneath it, three small stats in a row: `66% paper & film · 71% communication breakdown · 76% hearing loss`.
- **Why:** One shocking number beats four competing ones. "Zero of thirty-eight" is the most memorable thing in your pitch — let it own the screen. (Judging: **Desirability**.)

### Slide 5 · The solution — how it works
- **On screen:** *Collect first. Organise later.*
- **Visual:** Three panels left to right — **Capture → Timeline → Summary** — each with one hi-fi screen from your Figma file, phone-framed, and a two-word label. Use `04_design/hi-fiDraft/PhotoUpload.png` for Capture and the home timeline crop for Timeline.
- **Why:** Judges must be able to redraw your product from memory. Three panels, three verbs. (Judging: **Feasibility, Innovativeness**.)

### Slide 6 · The moment — the payoff
- **On screen:** *The record becomes the conversation.*
- **Visual:** The Summary screen, large, with one callout arrow: "every number taps back to its source film." If you can manage it, a 6–8 second silent screen recording of generating the summary — set to autoplay, no audio.
- **Why:** This is the emotional and functional climax; it earns its own slide. A moving prototype here does more for **Feasibility** than any architecture diagram.

### Slide 7 · Why nobody has done it
- **On screen:** *Patient-owned · Longitudinal · Accessibility-first · Consult-ready*
- **Visual:** A 2×2 or a four-column comparison: Hospital apps / Cloud drives / Record apps / **MediPort**, with ticks only in MediPort's column. Keep it to four rows.
- **Why:** Judges are explicitly scoring **Innovativeness** — don't make them infer it. Show the empty quadrant.

### Slide 8 · Feasibility
- **On screen:** *No new science. Six months. Three engineers.*
- **Visual:** Four small tech labels in a row (Camera · Cloud · OCR · LLM extraction), then a short timeline bar to MVP. Add a single line at the bottom in small type: *"We organise. We never diagnose."*
- **Why:** Pre-empts the two questions a judge is already forming — "can this actually be built?" and "is this a regulated medical device?" (Judging: **Feasibility**.)

### Slide 9 · The business
- **On screen:** *¥20 a month. One avoided scan pays for years.*
- **Visual:** Left — three revenue streams stacked (Subscription → Sponsored by insurers & patient orgs → Consented research data, marked *later*). Right — the expansion arrow: `NF2 → rare disease → any chronic patient with more than one hospital`.
- **Why:** The value comparison does the work; skip a market-size pyramid you can't defend under questioning. (Judging: **Viability**.)

### Slide 10 · The ask
- **On screen:** *What I need next: a clinician test, a patient-org pilot, a clinical partner.*
- **Visual:** Three clean items, your name and email at the bottom. Leave this slide up through the entire Q&A — it silently keeps your ask in front of the judges while they question you.
- **Why:** The brief explicitly invites you to ask for participation. Most finalists forget to. Be specific and small — specific asks get said yes to.

## Design system

| Element | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Palette** | One deep teal `#0F766E`, warm off-white `#FAF9F6`, near-black `#1F2933`, one accent for numbers only | Matches your report PDF; medical without being clinical-blue |
| **Type** | One serif for headlines (Lora — already your report face), one clean sans for everything else | Serif reads as considered, not startup-generic |
| **Size floor** | Nothing below 24pt. Headline lines 40–60pt | If it needs to be smaller, it belongs in your mouth, not on the slide |
| **Contrast** | 7:1 minimum | You're pitching accessibility — the deck itself is evidence. A judge who notices this scores it |
| **Density** | Max 12 words per slide | Your slides are captions, not documents |
| **Imagery** | Real photos of real records + your own hi-fi screens; zero stock photography | Stock imagery in a lived-experience pitch actively undercuts you |

## Delivery notes

**Rehearse to the clock, not the slides.** Say it out loud five times with a timer. The number to beat is 4:45. Most people speed up under pressure — mark a physical checkpoint: **if you are not on Slide 5 by 2:30, skip both ✂ lines.**

**Silence is a tool.** Three planned pauses: after "wearing a mask" (0:55), after "Zero." (1:55), after "The record becomes the conversation" (3:25). Two full beats each. They will feel far too long to you and exactly right to the room.

**Set up Q&A on your terms — before it starts.** At the end, right after "Thank you," add one calm line: *"I lip-read, so during questions could I ask you to face me — and I'll repeat each question back before I answer."* Say it as logistics, not apology. Repeating the question back is standard good pitch practice anyway; it buys you thinking time and confirms you heard correctly. Have a phone running live captions on the lectern as backup, and a notepad as backup to the backup.

**Prepare the five likely questions:**

1. *"How is this different from a hospital app or WeChat health record?"* → Those stop at one institution; the whole problem is what happens between institutions.
2. *"What if the AI extracts a value wrong?"* → Every number taps back to its source image, so errors are inspectable and correctable — that traceability is also why doctors trust it.
3. *"Is this a regulated medical device?"* → No. We organise and present the patient's own information; we never interpret or advise. That line is a deliberate design boundary, not an oversight.
4. *"Will patients really pay?"* → Caregivers are the payers, and the comparison isn't ¥20 versus zero — it's ¥20 versus a repeated MRI and a wasted cross-province trip. Willingness-to-pay testing is in my next validation round. *(Don't oversell — naming what you haven't yet tested reads as credible, not weak.)*
5. *"You're one person — can you build this?"* → I'm building the validated design; the MVP needs 2–3 engineers, which is what I'm seeking partners for. That's exactly the ask on the final slide.

**If a question stumps you,** say so plainly and say what you'd do to find out. Judges score composure, and "I don't know yet — here's how I'd test it" is a stronger answer than a confident guess.
